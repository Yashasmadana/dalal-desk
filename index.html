Best week in years.

Nifty is back above 25,000 for the first time since March. Oil cooled, the US vote went India's way, and foreign money came back. The question now is whether it lasts.

Sensex added more than 2,000 points on Thursday and another 1,718 on Friday to settle above 81,000, its biggest weekly gain in years.

The bounce comes at the end of a hard Samvat 2082. Nifty and Sensex logged their worst Samvat-year returns in 15 years. Foreign investors sold a record ₹4.55 trillion, and domestic funds bought a record amount to absorb it.

Muhurat trading opened Samvat 2083 on Sunday. Oil, the Iran war and foreign flows will decide whether this rally has legs.

What moved the market

US vote

Democrats won the midterms. Markets read it as less room for new tariffs and a better shot at an India–US trade deal.

Oil ▼13%

Brent fell toward $99 on US–Iran talk hopes. India imports most of its crude, so cheaper oil helps inflation, the rupee and margins.

FIIs return

Foreign buying came back after months of heavy selling. One good week does not yet make a trend.

    Business qualityEconomic moat

    A moat is a lasting advantage that stops competitors from eating a company's profits. Warren Buffett popularised the term.

    • Brand: customers pay more for the name (Pidilite's Fevicol, Titan).
    • Switching costs: leaving is painful (core banking software, a bank's salary accounts).
    • Network effects: each new user makes it better for others (Info Edge's Naukri, exchanges like BSE).
    • Cost advantage: cheaper at scale (HDFC Bank's low-cost deposits, DMart's lean stores).
    • Distribution & licences: reach or permissions rivals can't copy (Asian Paints' dealer network, IRCTC).

    The numbers test: a real moat shows up as ROCE well above the cost of capital, 15%+ for ten years or more, with stable margins through bad years.

    ProfitabilityEBITDA and EBITDA margin

    Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortisation. It shows what the core business earns before financing choices, tax and accounting for old investments.

    EBITDA = Revenue − operating costs
    EBITDA margin = EBITDA ÷ Revenue

    Revenue ₹1,000 cr, operating costs ₹780 cr → EBITDA ₹220 cr, margin 22%. Compare margins only within a sector: IT runs near 20–25%, retail far lower.

    Watch out: EBITDA is not cash. It ignores the capex a business must spend to stay alive, and interest on its debt. Heavy-debt or capital-hungry firms love quoting it.

    ValuationEnterprise value and EV/EBITDA

    Enterprise value is the price to buy the whole business, including its debts.

    EV = Market cap + Total debt − Cash
    EV/EBITDA = EV ÷ EBITDA

    Market cap ₹5,000 cr, debt ₹1,200 cr, cash ₹200 cr → EV ₹6,000 cr. With EBITDA of ₹600 cr, EV/EBITDA is 10×.

    Better than P/E for comparing companies with different debt levels, such as steel, telecom and power companies.

    ProfitabilityROE, ROCE and the DuPont breakdown
    ROE = Net profit ÷ Shareholders' equity
    ROCE = EBIT ÷ (Equity + Debt)

    ROE is the return on owners' money; ROCE is the return on all money in the business, so debt can't flatter it.

    DuPont splits ROE into three drivers:

    ROE = (Profit ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity)
         = Margin × Asset turnover × Leverage

    Two firms with 20% ROE can be very different: one earns it through fat margins, the other through borrowing. The second is riskier.

    CashFree cash flow
    FCF = Cash from operations − Capital expenditure

    The cash left after running and maintaining the business. It pays dividends, buys back shares and clears debt.

    Profit can be inflated by accounting choices; cash is harder to fake. A company reporting rising profits but negative FCF year after year deserves a closer look at receivables and inventory.

    ModellingDCF: valuing a business from its cash flows

    A Discounted Cash Flow model says a business is worth all its future free cash flows, brought back to today's money.

    Value = Σ FCFt ÷ (1 + r)t + TV ÷ (1 + r)n
    Terminal value TV = FCFn+1 ÷ (r − g)

    r = discount rate (WACC), g = long-run growth after year n. Try it:

    Lesson: with the defaults, about three-quarters of the value comes from the terminal value. Change r or g by 1% and watch the answer swing. A DCF is only as good as its assumptions.

    ModellingWACC and CAPM: the discount rate

    CAPM estimates the return shareholders demand:

    Cost of equity Ke = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf)

    Say the 10-year G-sec yields 7%, the stock's beta is 1.1, and the equity risk premium is 6%: Ke = 7 + 1.1 × 6 = 13.6%.

    WACC = (E ÷ V) × Ke + (D ÷ V) × Kd × (1 − tax)

    The weighted cost of all capital. A business creates value only when its ROCE beats its WACC.

    RiskBeta, volatility, Sharpe ratio and drawdown
    • Beta (β): how much a stock moves with the market. β 1.3 tends to move 1.3% when Nifty moves 1%.
    • Volatility (σ): standard deviation of returns. Nifty's long-run yearly σ is roughly 15–20%.
    • Sharpe ratio: return earned per unit of risk.
    Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) ÷ σp

    Fund A returns 15% with σ 20%; fund B returns 13% with σ 12%. With Rf 7%, A's Sharpe is 0.40 and B's is 0.50. B gives more return per unit of risk.

    Max drawdown: the worst fall from a peak to a trough. It tells you how much pain you'd have had to sit through.

    ModellingCAGR, XIRR and Monte Carlo
    CAGR = (End value ÷ Start value)1/years − 1

    ₹1 lakh growing to ₹2.5 lakh in 7 years is a CAGR of 14.0%.

    XIRR is the right measure when money goes in at different times, like a SIP. It finds the single rate that makes the present value of all your cash flows zero. Spreadsheets have an XIRR function.

    Monte Carlo simulation replaces "assume 12% every year" with thousands of random return paths built from an average and a volatility. Instead of one answer, you get odds, such as "an 80% chance of reaching ₹20 lakh in 10 years". It's how professionals stress-test retirement and goal plans.

    ValuationMargin of safety and operating leverage

    Margin of safety (Benjamin Graham): buy only when the price is well below your estimate of value, say 25–30% below a DCF value, so errors in your model don't sink you.

    Operating leverage: when most costs are fixed, a small rise in sales causes a big rise in profit, and the reverse in a downturn.

    Degree of operating leverage = % change in EBIT ÷ % change in sales

    A DOL of 3 means a 10% sales rise lifts operating profit about 30%. Airlines, cement and telecom carry high operating leverage.

    FundsWhat is a SIP?

    A Systematic Investment Plan puts a fixed amount into a mutual fund every month. When prices are low you buy more units, and when they are high you buy fewer. Over years this smooths out the ups and downs.

    The goal planner above shows how a SIP grows. Raising it 10% every year (a step-up) makes a big difference.

    FundsIndex fund or active fund?

    An index fund simply buys every stock in an index like Nifty 50. Fees are very low, often 0.1–0.3% a year.

    An active fund has a manager picking stocks and charges more, often 0.5–1.5% in direct plans. Many active funds fail to beat their index over long periods, which is why index funds are a good starting point.

    Reading stocksWhat is the P/E ratio?

    Price-to-earnings = share price ÷ profit per share. A P/E of 25 means you pay ₹25 for every ₹1 the company earns in a year.

    High P/E means investors expect fast growth, or the stock is expensive. Low P/E can mean cheap, or a business in trouble. Compare P/E only with similar companies in the same sector.

    Reading the newsWho are FIIs and DIIs?

    FIIs (foreign institutional investors) are overseas funds investing in India. DIIs are Indian mutual funds, insurers and pension funds.

    When FIIs sell heavily, the market and the rupee often fall. In Samvat 2082, FIIs sold a record ₹4.55 trillion while DIIs, fed by everyday SIPs, bought even more.

    Reading the newsWhy does oil move Indian stocks?

    India imports most of the crude oil it uses, paying in dollars. When oil rises, more dollars leave the country, the rupee weakens, fuel and transport costs push inflation up, and the RBI is less likely to cut rates.

    Paint, airline, tyre and oil-marketing companies are hit hardest. That's why the War section tracks Brent crude so closely.

    Reading the newsWhat does the RBI repo rate do?

    The repo rate is what the RBI charges banks to borrow. When it goes up, loan EMIs and FD rates usually rise, and stocks of banks, real estate and autos can feel pressure. When it goes down, the opposite tends to happen.

    The RBI's policy committee meets six times a year. The next dates are in the Calendar.

    MoneyHow are stock gains taxed?

    Under the rules in force since July 2024, for listed shares and equity funds:

    • Held over 12 months: long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year are taxed at 12.5%.
    • Held 12 months or less: short-term gains are taxed at 20%.

    Tax rules change with each Budget, so confirm the current rates with a tax adviser before you sell.

    MoneyMistakes beginners make
    • Trading futures & options. SEBI studies have found about 9 in 10 retail F&O traders lose money.
    • Buying on tips from Telegram, WhatsApp or "guaranteed return" schemes.
    • Stopping a SIP when markets fall, which is exactly when it buys cheapest.
    • Putting everything in one stock or one sector.
    • Checking prices every hour and reacting to every headline.
